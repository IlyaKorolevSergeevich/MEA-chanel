This work covers the design and simulation of a single readout channel for a transparent graphene microelectrode array (MEA) intended for chronic recording of retinal ganglion cell (RGC) activity with simultaneous visual stimulation. The channel consists of a graphene electrode modeled by a Randles circuit, a chopper-stabilized source follower for 1/f noise suppression, a low-pass filter for anti-aliasing and ripple rejection, and a programmable gain amplifier with a gain of 100 to match the ADC range.

The first stage was a Python noise model using NumPy and SciPy. Because graphene electrode parameters vary strongly between electrodes, the charge-transfer resistance and double-layer capacitance were treated as distributions rather than fixed values, and 2000 Monte Carlo simulations were run. This produced realistic and pessimistic scenarios and showed that flicker noise is the dominant contributor; without suppression, the SNR falls below the required level. The electrode was then represented in SPICE by a Randles equivalent circuit with Rct = 400 kΩ, which accounts for thermal noise, and Cdl = 180 pF, which captures the graphene–electrolyte interface. This made it possible to include the electrode’s own noise and to study its interaction with the CMOS input impedance.

At the transistor level, the design used the SKY130 130 nm process with standard nfet_01v8 and pfet_01v8 devices. These real BSIM4 models, with silicon-extracted parameters and realistic 1/f noise, were chosen because they allow a credible path to layout and tape-out. The baseline source follower showed 16.8 µV of noise in the 0.3–5 kHz band, which is too high for weak extracellular spikes and directly motivated chopper stabilization. The chopper switch, however, introduced charge injection: a real NMOS switch produced a 0.53 mV step. A dummy switch of half the size, driven in anti-phase, reduced this to 5.4 µV, showing that chopper stabilization is viable only when charge injection is explicitly compensated.

The final stages are a passive RC low-pass filter (10 kΩ, 265 pF) and a PGA with gain 100. The chopper modulates low-frequency noise to 200 kHz, and the LPF suppresses this transferred noise and provides anti-aliasing before digitization, while the PGA raises microvolt-level spikes to the hundreds-of-millivolts range expected by the ADC. A full-channel test with a 100 Hz signal and 5 mV interference confirmed that the chopper suppresses the disturbance while preserving the desired signal. The tools therefore played complementary roles: Python supported statistical noise analysis, while Xschem and ngspice with the SKY130 PDK enabled transistor-level verification of noise, charge injection, and chopper/LPF behavior.

The main lessons are that the architecture is viable at the noise-budget level, that chopper stabilization is necessary rather than optional, and that charge injection is a dominant practical issue that can be largely compensated with a dummy switch. In plain terms, the concept has been shown to simulate correctly from electrode model through the full readout chain. My next plan is to document the schematic in Xschem, draw the layout of one channel in KLayout, run DRC/LVS, then build the hierarchical 1024-channel array and prepare the design for an MPW tape-out. Therefore, the next steps will be aimed at ensuring that the channel can be drawn, tested, reproduced and, one day, sent for fabrication.
